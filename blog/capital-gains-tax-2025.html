Capital Gains Tax in India for FY 2026-27: Rates, Holding Periods, Exemptions and Worked Examples

Whenever you sell shares, mutual fund units, a house, land, gold or bonds for more than you paid, the profit is a capital gain and is taxed separately from your salary. How much tax you pay depends on two things: the type of asset and how long you held it. This guide brings together everything you need for FY 2026-27 (AY 2027-28) — current rates, holding periods, indexation on property, the reinvestment exemptions, loss set-off rules and two fully worked examples. You can check any figure with our Capital Gains Calculator.

How a capital gain is calculated

The basic formula is simple: Capital gain = Sale value − (Cost of acquisition + Cost of improvement + Transfer expenses). Transfer expenses include brokerage, demat charges and similar transaction costs, which can be subtracted from the sale value. Securities Transaction Tax (STT), however, is not deductible.

The gain is then classified as either short-term (STCG) or long-term (LTCG). The holding-period threshold differs by asset, so the same sale date can produce very different tax depending on what you sold.

These equity rates replaced the earlier regime of 15% STCG and 10% LTCG with a ₹1 lakh exemption. The current structure — 20% STCG, 12.5% LTCG and a ₹1.25 lakh exemption — continues to apply in FY 2026-27. The new Income-tax Act 2025, which applies from FY 2026-27, reorganises the law but keeps the existing capital gains rules.

Equity shares and equity mutual funds

Short-term gains (held up to 12 months)

Gains on listed shares and equity-oriented funds sold within 12 months are taxed at a flat 20%, regardless of your slab. There is no basic exemption specific to STCG, so frequent trading can become expensive.

Long-term gains and the ₹1.25 lakh exemption

Hold for more than 12 months and the gain becomes long-term. The first ₹1.25 lakh of long-term equity gains in a financial year is tax-free; only the excess is taxed at 12.5%. The allowance is annual and does not carry forward, so investors sitting on large unrealised gains often book a slice each year to use it rather than let it lapse.

For listed equity bought before 31 January 2018, the cost of acquisition is the higher of the actual cost or the fair market value on 31 January 2018 (the "grandfathering" rule), which protects gains made before that date.

Worked example 1: equity gains

Priya sells equity fund units after 13 months and makes a long-term gain of ₹5,00,000.

  • Exempt portion: ₹1,25,000
  • Taxable LTCG: ₹5,00,000 − ₹1,25,000 = ₹3,75,000
  • Tax at 12.5%: ₹3,75,000 × 12.5% = ₹46,875
  • Cess at 4%: ₹46,875 × 4% = ₹1,875
  • Total: ₹48,750

Under the older rules (10% above ₹1 lakh), the base tax on the same gain would have been (₹5,00,000 − ₹1,00,000) × 10% = ₹40,000. Had Priya sold after only eight months, a ₹2,00,000 short-term gain would cost ₹2,00,000 × 20% = ₹40,000 plus ₹1,600 cess, or ₹41,600 — a reminder that crossing the 12-month line matters.

Residential property and land

Property held for 24 months or less produces STCG, which is added to your income and taxed at your slab rate. Held for more than 24 months, the gain is long-term and taxed at 12.5% without indexation.

For land or buildings acquired before 23 July 2024 and sold after that date, you can choose the lower of two calculations: 20% with indexation or 12.5% without. Indexation raises your purchase cost for inflation using the Cost Inflation Index (CII) notified by the government:

Indexed cost = Purchase cost × (CII of sale year ÷ CII of purchase year)

Worked example 2: choosing indexation

Rakesh bought a flat for ₹40,00,000 and sells it for ₹90,00,000. For illustration, assume the CII ratio between the two years is 1.5, so his indexed cost is ₹40,00,000 × 1.5 = ₹60,00,000. (Use the actual notified CII values for your own sale.)

  • Without indexation: gain ₹90,00,000 − ₹40,00,000 = ₹50,00,000; tax at 12.5% = ₹6,25,000
  • With indexation: gain ₹90,00,000 − ₹60,00,000 = ₹30,00,000; tax at 20% = ₹6,00,000

Here indexation saves ₹25,000 before cess. The result flips if prices rose much faster than inflation, which is why it pays to run both options. If Rakesh also invested the full ₹30,00,000 indexed gain in Section 54EC bonds within six months, that gain would be exempt altogether.

Debt funds, bonds, gold and other assets

  • Specified debt mutual funds bought on or after 1 April 2023 are deemed short-term under Section 50AA no matter how long you hold them, so gains are taxed at your slab rate.
  • Bonds, jewellery and unlisted shares held for more than 24 months attract 12.5% LTCG; held for less, the gain is taxed at slab rates.
  • Non-exempt ULIPs — policies with an annual premium above ₹2.5 lakh — are taxed as capital gains, similar to equity funds, instead of enjoying full exemption.

Exemptions that can reduce or eliminate the tax

  • Section 54 (house to house): long-term gains from selling a residential house are exempt to the extent they are reinvested in another residential house — purchased within 2 years, or constructed within 3 years.
  • Section 54EC (capital gains bonds): invest up to ₹50 lakh of long-term gains from land or buildings in NHAI or REC bonds within 6 months of the sale.
  • Section 54F (other assets to house): long-term gains from non-residential assets — gold, mutual funds, commercial property — can be exempted by reinvesting the entire net sale consideration in a residential house.

All three carry strict time limits and conditions, so plan the reinvestment before you sell, not after.

Setting off and carrying forward losses

Short-term capital losses can be set off against both short-term and long-term gains. Long-term capital losses can be set off only against long-term gains. Unabsorbed losses can be carried forward for up to 8 assessment years — but only if you file your return on time for the year in which the loss arose. Missing that filing forfeits the carry-forward.

Practical strategies to lower your capital gains tax

  1. Track holding periods. Selling equity a few weeks after the 12-month mark can turn a 20% tax into 12.5% on only the gain above ₹1.25 lakh.
  2. Harvest the annual exemption. Book up to ₹1.25 lakh of long-term equity gains each year and reinvest, resetting your cost.
  3. Harvest losses. Realise losing positions to offset gains in the same year, keeping the 8-year carry-forward in mind.
  4. Compare indexation options on older property before choosing the 12.5% or 20% route.
  5. Use Sections 54, 54EC and 54F for property and other long-term gains, respecting the deadlines.
  6. Keep records of purchase deeds, improvement bills and brokerage statements to support your cost figures.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the capital gains tax rate on equity for FY 2026-27?
Listed equity shares and equity mutual funds held up to 12 months are taxed at a flat 20% STCG. Held for more than 12 months, gains are long-term and taxed at 12.5% on the amount above ₹1.25 lakh a year. Cess of 4% is added on top.
How does the ₹1.25 lakh LTCG exemption work?
The first ₹1.25 lakh of long-term gains from listed equity and equity funds in a financial year is tax-free. It is an annual allowance that does not carry forward, so many investors book some gains every year to use it.
Should I choose indexation when selling property?
For land or buildings acquired before 23 July 2024 and sold after that date, you can pick the lower of 20% with indexation or 12.5% without. Indexation usually wins when prices rose slowly relative to inflation; run both options in our calculator.
How are debt mutual funds taxed now?
Specified debt mutual funds bought on or after 1 April 2023 are treated as short-term under Section 50AA regardless of holding period, so gains are taxed at your income slab rate.
How can I save tax on gains from selling property?
Reinvest in another residential house under Section 54 (purchase within 2 years or construct within 3 years), invest up to ₹50 lakh in NHAI or REC bonds within 6 months under Section 54EC, or use Section 54F for gains from non-residential assets.
Can capital losses be carried forward?
Yes. Short-term losses can be set off against short- or long-term gains; long-term losses only against long-term gains. Unused losses can be carried forward for 8 assessment years if you file your return on time for the loss year.
