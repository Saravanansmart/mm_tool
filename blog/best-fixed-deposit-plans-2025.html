FD vs RD: A Practical Guide to Fixed and Recurring Deposits

Fixed deposits (FDs) and recurring deposits (RDs) are the two most common guaranteed-return savings products in India. Both lock in an interest rate when you book them, and both usually compound quarterly. The difference is how the money goes in: an FD takes one lump sum, while an RD takes a fixed amount every month. This guide explains how each works, compares them with real numbers and shows when each makes sense in FY 2026-27.

How a fixed deposit works

You deposit a lump sum for a fixed term, from 7 days up to 10 years, at a rate that stays the same until maturity. Most banks compound FD interest quarterly, so you earn interest on interest. Cumulative FDs reinvest interest until maturity. Non-cumulative FDs pay it out monthly, quarterly or annually, which suits people who need regular income.

The maturity formula is A = P × (1 + r/4)4t, where P is the principal, r the annual rate and t the tenure in years. For tenures under six months, banks generally apply simple interest instead.

  • Typical rates: about 6%–7.75% p.a. for general depositors. Senior citizens (60+) usually get an extra 0.25–0.75%.
  • Minimum deposit: banks typically start at ₹1,000, and post office time deposits at ₹100.
  • Safety: bank deposits are insured by DICGC up to ₹5 lakh per depositor per bank.

How a recurring deposit works

With an RD you commit to a fixed monthly instalment for 6 months to 10 years at a bank (5 years is standard at the post office). Each instalment earns interest at the booked rate, compounded quarterly, but only for the time it actually stays invested. The first instalment earns interest for the full tenure. The last one earns it for just a month.

  • Rates: generally the same as FD rates, about 6%–7.5% for general depositors, plus 0.25–0.75% for senior citizens.
  • Minimum instalment: ₹100 a month at most banks and post offices.
  • Missed instalments: banks usually charge a small penalty (about ₹1–₹2 per ₹100 per missed month), and repeated defaults can close the account.
  • Safety: bank RDs are covered by DICGC insurance up to ₹5 lakh, and post office RDs are sovereign-backed.

Worked example: the same ₹3 lakh, two ways

Suppose you'll set aside ₹3,00,000 over five years at 7% p.a., compounded quarterly.

Option A: RD of ₹5,000 a month for 60 months. You deposit ₹5,000 × 60 = ₹3,00,000. Each instalment grows at (1 + 0.07/4) for every quarter it has left. Added together, the RD matures at about ₹3,59,664, or roughly ₹59,664 of interest.

Option B: FD of ₹3,00,000 today for 5 years. Maturity = 3,00,000 × (1.0175)20 ≈ ₹4,24,433, or about ₹1,24,433 of interest.

The FD earns about twice the interest because all ₹3 lakh compounds from day one. In the RD, the average rupee is invested for only about half the tenure. That doesn't make the RD worse. It solves a different problem: most people don't have ₹3 lakh today, but they can save ₹5,000 from each salary. The RD turns that habit into a guaranteed corpus.

After tax: interest on both is taxed at your slab rate. For someone in the 30% slab (31.2% with cess), the RD's ₹59,664 of interest becomes about ₹41,049 after tax, and the FD's ₹1,24,433 becomes about ₹85,610. Run your own after-tax numbers before you compare a deposit with other options.

Does compounding frequency matter?

A little. A ₹5,00,000 FD at 7% for one year matures at about ₹5,35,930 with quarterly compounding, against ₹5,35,000 at simple interest. Over five years, quarterly compounding gives about ₹7,07,389, against ₹7,01,276 with yearly compounding. That's a gap of roughly ₹6,100. When comparing offers, look at the effective annual yield as well as the headline rate.

Tax, TDS and premature withdrawal

  • Interest is fully taxable at your slab rate, for both FDs and RDs. Banks deduct TDS once your annual interest crosses the threshold, and you can claim that TDS as a credit when you file your return.
  • Tax-saver FD: a 5-year tax-saver FD qualifies for a deduction of up to ₹1.5 lakh under Section 80C (old regime). It has a 5-year lock-in and no premature withdrawal.
  • Breaking a deposit early: banks usually cut 0.5–1% from the applicable rate. The effective rate is the lower of your booking rate and the card rate for the period you actually stayed invested. If you might need the money early, a shorter deposit is usually better than breaking a long one.

Smart strategies

  • Ladder your FDs. Rather than locking ₹15 lakh into one deposit, split it into three ₹5 lakh FDs of 1, 3 and 5 years. Money comes free at regular intervals, and part of it still earns longer-term rates. Renew each tranche as it matures.
  • Use an RD for a dated goal. A 1–2 year RD fits a holiday, a gadget or a car down payment. Match the tenure to the goal date so you never have to break it.
  • Combine both. Many savers park their emergency fund and any lump sums (bonus, maturity proceeds) in FDs, and send monthly surplus to an RD.
  • Compare rates across banks. Smaller private and small finance banks often pay 0.5–1% more than the largest banks. Stay within the ₹5 lakh DICGC cover per bank.
  • Check the real return. With 6–7% inflation, the real return on a 7% FD is close to zero before tax. Deposits suit safety and short-term goals, not long-term wealth building.

Which should you choose?

Ask yourself one question: is the money already in your account, or will it arrive month by month?

  • Choose an FD if you have a lump sum: a bonus, an inheritance, the proceeds of a matured investment, or an emergency fund you want earning more than a savings account. A non-cumulative FD can also provide regular income for retirees.
  • Choose an RD if you want to build a sum from your salary and need the discipline of a fixed monthly debit. It's especially useful for first-time savers and for goals one to three years away.
  • Choose neither for goals more than five to seven years away. After tax and inflation, deposits barely grow in real terms. Equity mutual funds, PPF or NPS are usually better long-term tools, with deposits for the part you can't afford to see fall.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which earns more, an FD or an RD?
For the same total amount, rate and tenure, an FD earns more because the whole sum compounds from day one. For example, ₹3 lakh at 7% for 5 years grows to about ₹4,24,433 as an FD, versus about ₹3,59,664 as a ₹5,000-a-month RD. An RD is the better fit when you save from monthly income rather than holding a lump sum.
Do senior citizens get higher FD and RD rates?
Yes. Most banks offer senior citizens (age 60+) an extra 0.25-0.75% p.a. over the standard rate on the same tenure, for both FDs and RDs.
Is FD and RD interest taxable?
Yes. Interest on both is fully taxable at your income tax slab rate, and banks deduct TDS once annual interest crosses the threshold. Only the 5-year tax-saver FD offers a Section 80C deduction, under the old regime.
What happens if I withdraw an FD or RD before maturity?
Premature closure is usually allowed but banks typically cut 0.5-1% from the applicable rate. Tax-saver FDs cannot be withdrawn before 5 years.
What is the minimum amount to start an FD or RD?
Banks typically accept FDs from ₹1,000 and post office time deposits from ₹100. RDs can start from ₹100 a month at most banks and post offices.
What is FD laddering?
Splitting a lump sum across several tenures, for example dividing ₹15 lakh into 1-year, 3-year and 5-year FDs, so money becomes available at regular intervals while part of it still earns longer-term rates.
