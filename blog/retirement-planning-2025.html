Retirement Planning in India: Corpus, Investments and Safety Net

A comfortable retirement comes down to three questions. How much will you need? Where will that money come from? And what protects your family if something goes wrong before you get there? This guide works through each one for FY 2026-27, with a worked corpus calculation, the main retirement products available in India, and the low-cost government schemes that act as a basic safety net.

Step 1: Estimate your retirement expenses

Start with what you spend each month today, leaving out costs that end at retirement (home loan EMIs, children's school fees) and adding ones that grow (healthcare, travel). Then inflate it to your retirement year. At 6% a year, about India's long-term average, prices roughly double every 12 years. The formula is simple: future expense = today's expense × (1 + inflation)years.

Healthcare usually rises faster than general prices, so treat health insurance as part of the plan, not an extra.

Step 2: Set a corpus target

A common guideline is a corpus of 25–30 times your annual expenses at retirement. That's large enough to fund a long retirement if it stays invested sensibly. A more precise method, which our Retirement Planning Calculator uses, works out the monthly withdrawal that would run the corpus down to zero over your expected years in retirement. For a balanced portfolio, 9–11% a year before retirement and 7–8% after is a defensible long-term assumption for India.

Worked example: a 35-year-old spending ₹50,000 a month

  1. Inflate expenses: ₹50,000 × (1.06)25 ≈ ₹2,14,594 a month at age 60. That's about ₹25.75 lakh a year.
  2. Apply the 25× rule: ₹25.75 lakh × 25 ≈ ₹6.44 crore target corpus.
  3. Work out the monthly saving: to build ₹6.44 crore in 25 years (300 months), you'd need to invest about ₹48,100 a month at 10%, or about ₹33,900 a month at 12%.

That looks steep, but it's before counting what you already have. EPF contributions, an existing NPS balance and any current investments all reduce the gap. A step-up also helps a lot: start lower and raise your SIP and NPS contributions by about 10% each year as your salary grows. Time matters most. ₹15,000 a month for 30 years at 12% grows to roughly ₹5.3 crore, so every year you start earlier sharply reduces the monthly amount needed.

Step 3: Choose your retirement investments

  • EPF: the backbone for most salaried employees. You and your employer each contribute 12% of basic + DA, with part of the employer's share going to the Employees' Pension Scheme. The EPFO-declared rate is 8.25% p.a. for FY 2025-26. Project your balance with the EPF Calculator.
  • NPS: a low-cost, market-linked pension scheme regulated by PFRDA. Historical long-term returns for equity-heavy allocations have been about 9–12%, though returns aren't guaranteed. At retirement you can withdraw up to 60% of the corpus tax-free and use at least 40% to buy an annuity. Under the old regime, contributions qualify under Section 80C plus an extra ₹50,000 under Section 80CCD(1B). Employer contributions are deductible under Section 80CCD(2) in both regimes.
  • PPF: government-backed and tax-free at 7.1% p.a., with a 15-year lock-in and Section 80C eligibility. It's a good fit for the stable debt portion of the plan.
  • Equity mutual fund SIPs: the main growth engine over long horizons. Planners commonly assume 10–12% long-term returns, with year-to-year ups and downs.
  • Fixed deposits: useful for short-term safety and, after retirement, for the next few years' expenses. Not a long-term growth tool.

NPS example: ₹5,000 a month from age 30 to 60 at 10% builds a corpus of about ₹1.14 crore. With 40% used for an annuity at 6%, that gives a tax-free lump sum of about ₹68 lakh and a monthly pension of about ₹22,800. Try your own numbers with the NPS Calculator.

Asset allocation

One common starting mix while you're working is roughly 60% equity, 30% debt and 10% liquid. Shift gradually towards debt in the decade before retirement so a market fall just before you stop working doesn't derail the plan. Review once a year, rebalance, and raise contributions as your income rises.

After retirement, a simple bucket approach works well. Keep the next few years' expenses in safe, liquid options such as FDs, the medium term in debt, and leave a portion in equity so the corpus keeps growing faster than inflation over a retirement that could last 25–30 years.

Step 4: Build a safety net with government schemes

A retirement plan can fail if a death, accident or disability strikes during the earning years. Three low-cost government schemes, known together as the Jan Suraksha schemes, provide a basic layer of protection. They're especially useful for unorganised-sector workers and low-income families:

  • How to enrol: through your bank, with the premium or contribution auto-debited from your savings account each year (or each month for APY). A Jan Dhan (PMJDY) account works for this, and renewal is automatic as long as the balance covers the debit.
  • Where they fit: ₹2 lakh of cover is a floor, not a full plan.   • APY's role: APY offers a fixed, guaranteed pension, which complements the market-linked growth of NPS and mutual funds. Check eligibility and the contribution for your entry age with your bank before enrolling.

Common retirement planning mistakes

  • Starting late. Compounding needs time. Waiting ten years can roughly double or triple the monthly saving you'd need for the same corpus.
  • Ignoring inflation. A target that looks large today, such as ₹1 crore, buys far less in 25 years. Always work in future rupees.
  • Withdrawing EPF when you change jobs. Transfer the balance instead, so it keeps compounding.
  • Relying only on fixed-income products. FDs and savings accounts alone rarely beat inflation after tax over several decades.
  • Skipping insurance. One uninsured hospital stay or the loss of the main earner can wipe out years of saving. Protection comes before aggressive investing, not after.

Frequently Asked Questions

How large a retirement corpus do I need?
A common guideline is 25-30 times your annual expenses at retirement. For example, someone spending ₹50,000 a month today will need about ₹2.15 lakh a month in 25 years at 6% inflation, which points to a corpus of roughly ₹6.4 crore using the 25x rule.
How much should I save every month for retirement?
It depends on your age, expenses, existing savings and expected returns. In our example, building ₹6.44 crore over 25 years needs about ₹48,100 a month at 10% or ₹33,900 at 12%, before counting EPF or other existing savings. Starting earlier and stepping up contributions every year reduces the amount sharply.
Which investments are best for retirement in India?
Most plans combine EPF (8.25% for FY 2025-26), NPS for market-linked growth with extra tax benefits, PPF at 7.1% tax-free for stable debt, and equity mutual fund SIPs for long-term growth, with fixed deposits for short-term needs.
What do PMJJBY and PMSBY cover?
PMJJBY gives ₹2 lakh of life cover for those aged 18-50 at ₹436 a year. PMSBY gives ₹2 lakh of accidental death or full disability cover for ages 18-70 at ₹20 a year. Both are renewed by auto-debit from your bank account.
What pension does Atal Pension Yojana guarantee?
Atal Pension Yojana guarantees a monthly pension of ₹1,000 to ₹5,000 from age 60, depending on the pension level you choose and your contributions. Enrolment and auto-debit are handled through your bank.
What asset allocation suits a retirement portfolio?
A common starting point while working is about 60% equity, 30% debt and 10% liquid, gradually shifting towards debt in the decade before retirement and rebalancing once a year.
