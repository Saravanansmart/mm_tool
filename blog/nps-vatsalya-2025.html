NPS Vatsalya Explained: Tax Benefits, Rules and Returns for Your Child (FY 2026-27)

NPS Vatsalya lets a parent or guardian open a National Pension System (NPS) account in a child's name and start investing for that child's retirement decades before they earn their first salary. Launched in 2024 and given a tax boost in Budget 2025, it is one of the few products that rewards very early, very long-term compounding. This guide explains how the scheme works, what the tax benefit is actually worth, what a realistic projection looks like, and where it fits alongside other savings for your child in FY 2026-27.

What Is NPS Vatsalya?

NPS Vatsalya is an NPS account opened for a minor (anyone below 18) and operated by a parent or guardian. The child is the account holder and beneficiary; the guardian makes contributions and handles the paperwork until the child comes of age. Like the regular National Pension System, it falls under the Pension Fund Regulatory and Development Authority (PFRDA), and the money is invested in market-linked funds through professional fund managers.

The key design feature is continuity. When the child turns 18, the account converts into a regular NPS account in their own name. Nothing is cashed out or restarted — the corpus built during childhood simply keeps compounding under the adult NPS structure, and the young adult can continue contributing from there.

Key Features at a Glance

  • Who can open it: A parent or guardian, for a child below 18.
  • Minimum contribution: ₹1,000 a year. Above that, amounts are flexible and can be paid monthly, quarterly or as one-off top-ups.
  • Investment choice: Contributions go into equity and debt allocations, so you can tilt towards higher growth potential while the time horizon is long.
  • At 18: The account automatically converts into a regular NPS account in the child's name.
  • At 60: Standard NPS rules apply — up to 60% of the corpus can be withdrawn as a tax-free lump sum, and at least 40% must buy an annuity that pays a monthly pension. The pension itself is taxable at the person's slab rate.
  • Returns: Market-linked and not guaranteed. Long-term NPS returns have historically been in the 9–12% range for equity-heavy allocations, but past performance does not promise future returns.

Tax Benefit: What Budget 2025 Changed

Budget 2025 extended the additional deduction under Section 80CCD(1B) — up to ₹50,000 a year — to contributions a parent makes to a minor's NPS Vatsalya account. This deduction sits over and above the ₹1.5 lakh Section 80C limit.

There is one important condition: this deduction is available only under the Old Tax Regime. Under the New Regime (the default under the Income Tax Act 2025), most Chapter VI-A deductions are not allowed; the NPS-related deduction it does permit is the employer contribution under Section 80CCD(2), not 80CCD(1B). If you have opted for the New Regime, NPS Vatsalya still works as a long-term investment, but it will not reduce your tax.

What the deduction is worth depends on your slab. Including the 4% health and education cess:

  • 30% slab: ₹50,000 × 30% = ₹15,000, plus 4% cess (₹600) = ₹15,600 saved a year.
  • 20% slab: ₹50,000 × 20% = ₹10,000, plus 4% cess (₹400) = ₹10,400 a year.
  • 5% slab: ₹50,000 × 5% = ₹2,500, plus 4% cess (₹100) = ₹2,600 a year.

Before you plan around the deduction, confirm how the ₹50,000 limit interacts with any NPS contributions you already make for yourself, since 80CCD(1B) is one section with one cap per taxpayer. A tax professional can confirm this for your situation.

Worked Example: ₹5,000 a Month From Age 5 to 18

Suppose you contribute ₹5,000 a month from your child's 5th birthday until they turn 18 — 13 years, or 156 monthly contributions — and the portfolio earns 10% a year. NPS projections use the future value of an annuity-due with monthly compounding:

FV = R × [((1 + i)n − 1) / i] × (1 + i)

  • R = ₹5,000; i = 10% ÷ 12 = 0.8333% a month (0.008333); n = 156 months
  • (1.008333)156 ≈ 3.6496
  • FV = 5,000 × [(3.6496 − 1) ÷ 0.008333] × 1.008333 = 5,000 × 317.95 × 1.008333 ≈ ₹16.03 lakh

You would have put in ₹7.8 lakh (₹5,000 × 156), so roughly ₹8.23 lakh of the corpus at 18 is growth. You contribute ₹60,000 a year, but only ₹50,000 counts for 80CCD(1B). In the 30% slab that is ₹15,600 saved each year, or about ₹2.03 lakh (₹15,600 × 13) over the 13 years, if you stay in the Old Regime throughout.

How much starting earlier matters, at the same ₹5,000 a month and 10% return:

  • From birth (18 years): invested ₹10.8 lakh → about ₹30.28 lakh at 18
  • From age 5 (13 years): invested ₹7.8 lakh → about ₹16.03 lakh at 18
  • From age 10 (8 years): invested ₹4.8 lakh → about ₹7.37 lakh at 18

Starting at birth rather than age 10 more than doubles what you put in, but it roughly quadruples the corpus. That is the core case for NPS Vatsalya: the child's account gets the one thing an adult can never buy back — extra decades of compounding.

If the ₹16.03 lakh from the age-5 example were left untouched from 18 to 60 at the same 10% assumption, it would grow about 65.5-fold (1.008333504) to roughly ₹10.5 crore. Treat that as an illustration of compounding, not a forecast: returns will vary, and inflation over 42 years will make that number buy far less than it does today.

Where NPS Vatsalya Fits in Your Child's Plan

Because the account becomes a regular NPS Tier I account at 18, the money is meant for retirement, and Tier I is locked in until 60 apart from limited partial withdrawals for specific needs. That makes NPS Vatsalya a poor vehicle for goals that fall in the next 10–20 years, such as college fees or a wedding. For those, use goal-specific savings and plan them separately with our Child Education Corpus Calculator, or consider a Sukanya Samriddhi account if you have a daughter.

A sensible order of priorities for most families:

  1. Secure your own retirement and term cover first — a child's pension is not a substitute for your own.
  2. Fund near-term child goals (education) with appropriate instruments.
  3. Then use NPS Vatsalya for the long-term "head start", especially if you are in the Old Regime and can use the ₹50,000 deduction.

Compared with PPF, which offers guaranteed, tax-free returns of around 7.1%, NPS Vatsalya is market-linked: potentially higher long-run growth, but with no guarantee and a much longer lock-in.

How to Open an NPS Vatsalya Account

  • Where: Through banks, post offices, or the e-NPS portal.
  • Documents: The child's birth certificate (as proof of age) and the guardian's KYC documents.
  • First contribution: Make at least the ₹1,000 annual minimum to keep the account active, then set up a regular contribution that fits your budget.
  • Choose an allocation: With 13–18 years before the child even turns 18, a growth-oriented allocation is common, but match it to your comfort with market swings.

Exit, partial-withdrawal and conversion rules are set by PFRDA and can be revised. Check the current rules on the official NPS or PFRDA website before you rely on early access to the money.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is NPS Vatsalya?
It is an NPS account a parent or guardian opens in the name of a child below 18. Launched in 2024, it converts automatically into a regular NPS account when the child turns 18, so the corpus keeps compounding into adulthood.
What is the minimum contribution for NPS Vatsalya?
₹1,000 a year. Above that, contributions are flexible and can be made monthly, quarterly or as one-off top-ups.
What tax benefit do parents get on NPS Vatsalya contributions?
Budget 2025 extended the Section 80CCD(1B) deduction of up to ₹50,000 to contributions for minors, over and above the ₹1.5 lakh 80C limit. It is available only under the Old Tax Regime and is worth up to ₹15,600 a year in the 30% slab, including cess.
How much can ₹5,000 a month in NPS Vatsalya grow to?
Invested from age 5 to 18 at an assumed 10% return, ₹5,000 a month (₹7.8 lakh in total) grows to about ₹16.03 lakh by the time the child turns 18. Starting at birth, the same amount grows to about ₹30.28 lakh.
What happens to the account when the child turns 18?
It converts into a regular NPS account in the child's name. At 60, standard NPS rules apply: up to 60% can be withdrawn as a tax-free lump sum and at least 40% buys an annuity whose pension is taxable at slab rates.
Should I use NPS Vatsalya to save for my child's education?
Usually not. The money becomes a Tier I NPS account locked in until 60 apart from limited partial withdrawals, so it is designed for retirement. Plan education and other near-term goals with separate, more accessible savings.
