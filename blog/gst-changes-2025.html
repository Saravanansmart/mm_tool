GST 2.0 Explained: The New 5%, 18% and 40% Slabs, What Changed and What It Means for You

In September 2025, the 56th meeting of the GST Council approved the biggest overhaul of India's Goods and Services Tax since its launch in July 2017. Popularly called GST 2.0, the rate rationalisation came into force on 22 September 2025. It collapsed the old four-slab structure into a simpler system, cut rates on a wide range of everyday goods and services, and introduced new conditions on input tax credit (ITC) for some concessional rates. More than a year on, this guide explains the new structure, works through what the change means on an actual bill, and lists what businesses should have in place for FY 2026-27.

A Quick Refresher: How GST Works

GST replaced VAT, service tax, excise duty and a host of other central and state levies. It is a destination-based, multi-stage tax: each business in the chain pays tax on its sales but claims credit (ITC) for the GST it paid on purchases, so in effect it pays tax only on the value it adds. Depending on the supply, GST is collected as:

  • CGST + SGST/UTGST on intra-state supplies, split equally between the Centre and the state.
  • IGST on inter-state supplies and imports, collected in full by the Centre.

The New GST Slab Structure

Before the reform, most goods and services fell into one of four main slabs — 5%, 12%, 18% and 28% — with an additional compensation cess on many luxury and sin goods. GST 2.0 removed the 12% and 28% slabs and moved to a two-tier structure with a separate higher rate:

  • 0% (Nil): Unchanged for exempt items such as fresh food and many healthcare and education services.
  • 5% (merit rate): Essentials and many everyday household items. A large number of goods that were earlier taxed at 12% moved down to 5%.
  • 18% (standard rate): Most goods and services. Several consumer durables previously taxed at 28% moved down to 18%.
  • 40% (de-merit rate): A new special rate for luxury and sin goods.
  • 3%: Gold, silver and other precious metals, unchanged.

Classification is item-specific. The same broad category can contain products at different rates, so always confirm the rate for your exact HSN or SAC code in the official notifications rather than relying on a general list.

Why the Government Simplified GST

The multi-slab structure had become a source of classification disputes, with similar products taxed at different rates, and of compliance cost for small businesses. A two-tier structure makes it easier to price goods, reduces disputes over which slab applies, and lowers the tax burden on mass-consumption items. The trade-off was a higher, separate rate for luxury and sin goods.

Worked Examples: What GST 2.0 Means on a Bill

Example 1: An item that moved from 12% to 5%

Take a product with a pre-tax price of ₹1,000.

  • Old: ₹1,000 + 12% GST (₹120) = ₹1,120
  • New: ₹1,000 + 5% GST (₹50) = ₹1,050
  • Saving: ₹70 on every unit, or 6.25% of the old price (₹70 ÷ ₹1,120)

Example 2: A consumer durable that moved from 28% to 18%

Take an appliance with a pre-tax price of ₹50,000.

  • Old: ₹50,000 + 28% GST (₹14,000) = ₹64,000
  • New: ₹50,000 + 18% GST (₹9,000) = ₹59,000
  • Saving: ₹5,000, or about 7.8% of the old price (₹5,000 ÷ ₹64,000)

These savings reach the consumer only if the seller passes on the lower tax. Retailers were asked to update POS systems, e-commerce listings and MRPs to show revised prices, so check your bill for the new rate.

Example 3: Splitting GST on an invoice

On a ₹10,000 taxable supply at 18%, GST is ₹1,800 and the invoice total is ₹11,800. For an intra-state sale, this appears as CGST ₹900 + SGST ₹900. For an inter-state sale, the full ₹1,800 is charged as IGST. To work backwards from a GST-inclusive price, divide by (1 + rate): ₹11,800 ÷ 1.18 = ₹10,000 base, so GST = ₹1,800.

Input Tax Credit and Transition Rules

The rate cuts came with conditions that matter for businesses:

  • ITC restrictions on some concessional rates: For certain goods and services, the lower rate is available only if the supplier does not claim input tax credit. Check whether each concessional rate you use carries this condition before claiming credit.
  • Transitional treatment: The GST Council's FAQs explain how to treat ITC already availed and transitional credits for supplies made up to 21 September 2025. Reconcile your ledgers and outward liabilities against that guidance, especially for stock purchased at the old rates and sold at the new ones.
  • Inverted duty structure: Where rate cuts on outputs left some businesses paying more tax on inputs than on sales, the government announced liquidity measures, including provisional refunds, to ease the transition for affected sectors.

Business Checklist for FY 2026-27

  1. Verify your rate master: Confirm every product and service in your billing system carries the correct post-reform rate and HSN/SAC code. Errors made in September 2025 can persist unnoticed for months.
  2. Check supplier invoices: If a supplier reduced its rate but you had earlier claimed ITC at the old rate, review the transitional guidance and adjust purchase accounting.
  3. Reconcile GSTR-2B every month: ITC is available only if the supplier has filed GSTR-1 and the invoice appears in your GSTR-2B. Some credits remain blocked under Section 17(5).
  4. Review inverted-duty positions: If your input rates are now higher than your output rates, check eligibility for refunds.
  5. File GSTR-3B on time: The due date is the 20th, 22nd or 24th of the following month depending on turnover. Late filing attracts a late fee of ₹50 a day (₹25 CGST + ₹25 SGST), or ₹20 a day for Nil returns, capped by turnover, plus interest at 18% a year on the net cash liability under Section 50 of the CGST Act.
  6. Keep prices and listings current: Make sure MRPs, catalogues and online listings reflect the correct rates.

Late-fee example

A regular taxpayer files GSTR-3B 10 days late with ₹1,00,000 of tax payable in cash. Late fee: ₹50 × 10 days = ₹500. Interest: ₹1,00,000 × 18% × 10 ÷ 365 ≈ ₹493. Total cost of the delay: about ₹993, before any other consequences.

What GST 2.0 Means for Consumers

For households, the main effect is lower tax on many essentials and on several consumer durables that moved from 28% to 18%, which should mean lower retail prices where sellers pass on the benefit. Luxury and sin goods now sit in the separate 40% rate. When you buy, the tax rate printed on your invoice tells you whether the revised rate has been applied.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is GST 2.0?
GST 2.0 is the rate rationalisation approved at the 56th GST Council meeting in September 2025 and effective from 22 September 2025. It simplified the slab structure and reduced rates on many everyday goods and services.
What are the GST slabs after GST 2.0?
The main rates are 5% (merit rate) and 18% (standard rate), with a separate 40% de-merit rate for luxury and sin goods. The 12% and 28% slabs were removed. Nil-rated items stay at 0% and gold and silver stay at 3%.
How much does a cut from 12% to 5% save?
On an item with a pre-tax price of ₹1,000, the price including GST falls from ₹1,120 to ₹1,050 — a saving of ₹70, or 6.25% of the old price, if the seller passes the cut on.
Does GST 2.0 affect input tax credit?
Yes. Some concessional rates are available only where input tax credit is not claimed, and the GST Council's FAQs explain how to treat ITC already availed and transitional credits for supplies made up to 21 September 2025.
What relief exists for businesses with an inverted duty structure?
The government announced measures, including provisional refunds, to protect liquidity for sectors where rate cuts left input tax higher than output tax.
Where can I find the official GST 2.0 notifications?
The PIB press note on the GST reforms and the GST Council's FAQ document on the 56th meeting decisions are the primary official sources, linked at the end of this guide.
