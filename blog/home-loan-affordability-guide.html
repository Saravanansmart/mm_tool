How Much Home Loan Can I Afford? EMI, FOIR and Tenure Guide

Banks don't start with how much you want to borrow. They work out how much you're allowed to borrow, using your income, your existing debts, the interest rate and the tenure. This guide walks through that maths for FY 2026-27, the levers that change the answer, and how to plan EMIs when you already have other loans. Every figure below uses the standard reducing-balance EMI formula, the same one behind our Home Loan EMI Calculator.

How lenders decide: the FOIR test

The key number is FOIR (Fixed Obligation to Income Ratio). It's your total monthly loan and credit obligations, including the new home loan EMI, as a share of your net take-home income. If you take home ₹1,00,000 a month and the lender's FOIR cap is 40%, all your EMIs together can't go above ₹40,000.

FOIR limits vary by lender and by profile. Lenders may go up to 50–60% of income for some borrowers, but keeping your total EMI at or below 40% of net income is the safer planning benchmark. It leaves room for living costs, savings and emergencies. We use 40% throughout this guide.

Maximum loan at 40% FOIR

Using a representative home loan rate of 8.5% p.a. (home loans for prime borrowers typically sit in the 8.5%–9.5% range) and a 20-year tenure, with no existing EMIs:

Net Monthly IncomeMax EMI (40%)Max Loan Eligible
₹50,000₹20,000₹23,04,617
₹1,00,000₹40,000₹46,09,234
₹2,00,000₹80,000₹92,18,467

These figures scale in proportion: at the same rate and tenure, double the income means double the eligible loan. The eligible loan is just the present value of the maximum EMI. In other words, it's the principal that the EMI fully repays over 240 months at 8.5%.

Worked example: a ₹1 lakh take-home salary with a car loan

Take Priya, whose net salary is ₹1,00,000 a month. She already pays a ₹10,000 car loan EMI and wants to buy a flat.

  1. FOIR ceiling: 40% × ₹1,00,000 = ₹40,000 of total EMIs allowed.
  2. Subtract existing EMIs: ₹40,000 − ₹10,000 = ₹30,000 left for the home loan EMI.
  3. Convert the EMI to a loan amount: at 8.5% over 20 years, a ₹30,000 EMI supports a loan of about ₹34,56,925.
  4. Compare with no car loan: with the full ₹40,000 available, she'd be eligible for ₹46,09,234.

A single ₹10,000 monthly commitment cuts her eligibility by ₹11,52,309, which is 25%. That's why clearing a small car or personal loan before applying can raise the amount a bank will sanction. Use the Loan Prepayment Calculator to check whether paying it off first is worth it. Also remember the bank won't fund the whole property price. Budget your down payment plus stamp duty and registration separately (our Stamp Duty Calculator estimates these by state).

Tenure: the biggest lever, and its cost

A longer tenure lowers the EMI for the same loan, so the same ₹40,000 EMI supports a bigger loan. At 8.5%:

TenureLoan a ₹40,000 EMI supports
15 years₹40,61,988
20 years₹46,09,234
25 years₹49,67,543
30 years₹52,02,146

The catch is total interest. On a ₹50 lakh loan at 8.5%, the EMI is ₹49,237 over 15 years (about ₹38.6 lakh of total interest), ₹43,391 over 20 years (about ₹54.1 lakh) and ₹40,261 over 25 years (about ₹70.8 lakh). Stretching from 15 to 25 years cuts the EMI by under ₹9,000 a month but adds roughly ₹32 lakh of interest. Going the other way pays off too: cutting a ₹30 lakh loan at 9% from 20 to 15 years raises the EMI from ₹26,992 to ₹30,428 but saves about ₹10 lakh in interest.

A practical approach is to take a tenure that keeps today's EMI comfortable. Then step the EMI up by 5–10% a year as your salary rises, or make part-prepayments, so the loan closes early.

Interest rate sensitivity

The rate you're offered also changes eligibility. For a ₹40,000 EMI over 20 years, the supported loan is about ₹47.8 lakh at 8%, ₹46.1 lakh at 8.5% and ₹44.5 lakh at 9%. Your credit score doesn't change the FOIR percentage, but it does affect the rate. Borrowers with a score above 750 generally get the best published rates, and a lower rate means a larger loan for the same EMI. Self-employed borrowers may pay 0.25–0.5% more than salaried borrowers.

Planning EMIs when you have more than one loan

FOIR counts every fixed obligation, so plan your loans as one budget, not one at a time:

  • Personal loans: these carry higher rates than home loans and have no tax benefit. Use them for genuine emergencies and repay them early. Tenure matters here too: a ₹20 lakh personal loan at 11% costs ₹43,485 a month over 5 years or ₹34,245 over 7 years. The longer option adds about ₹2.7 lakh of interest.
  • Education loans: interest paid qualifies for a deduction under Section 80E (old regime) for up to 8 years. Model the moratorium and EMI with the Education Loan Calculator.
  • Compare the full cost, not just the headline rate: check processing fees and prepayment terms before signing.
  • Keep an emergency fund separate: don't drain savings to make the down payment. A few months of EMIs and expenses set aside protects your credit score if income is interrupted. See our Emergency Fund Calculator.

Prepayment and tax benefits

Under RBI rules, floating-rate home loans to individuals carry no prepayment penalty. Fixed-rate loans may charge 2–3% of the outstanding principal. Prepayments in the first five years save the most interest, because early EMIs are mostly interest.

If you file under the old tax regime, you can deduct up to ₹2 lakh a year of interest on a self-occupied home under Section 24(b), and up to ₹1.5 lakh of principal repayment under Section 80C. Co-borrowers who are also co-owners and share the EMIs can each claim these limits. Most home loan deductions aren't available under the new regime, so compare both with the Income Tax Calculator before counting on the saving.

Common affordability mistakes

  • Borrowing the maximum the bank offers. Your sanction letter shows the most a lender will risk, not what fits your budget. If you'd still have to cover school fees, insurance premiums and SIPs, aim below the ceiling.
  • Planning around today's rate. Most home loans are floating-rate and linked to the RBI repo rate. When rates rise, the bank usually lengthens the tenure or raises the EMI. Make sure you could handle either.
  • Forgetting the one-time costs. Down payment, stamp duty, registration, interiors and moving costs come before the first EMI, and the bank finances none of them.
  • Ignoring a co-applicant. Adding an earning spouse as co-applicant adds their income to the FOIR base, which directly raises the eligible amount. If they're also a co-owner, both of you can claim the tax benefits.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much home loan can I get on a ₹1,00,000 monthly salary?
At a 40% FOIR, 8.5% interest and a 20-year tenure, roughly ₹46 lakh, assuming no other EMIs. An existing ₹10,000 EMI brings this down to about ₹34.6 lakh. The exact figure varies by lender and credit profile.
What is FOIR and why does it matter?
FOIR (Fixed Obligation to Income Ratio) is the share of your net monthly income that goes to all loan and credit obligations combined, including the new home loan EMI. Lenders use it to cap how much they will lend; 40% is a safe planning benchmark, though some lenders allow more.
Do existing loans reduce my home loan eligibility?
Yes, directly. On a ₹1,00,000 income, an existing ₹10,000 monthly EMI reduces the eligible home loan by about 25%, because it uses up part of the same FOIR limit the new EMI would otherwise use.
Does a longer loan tenure increase how much I can borrow?
Yes. A longer tenure lowers the EMI for the same loan, so a ₹40,000 EMI at 8.5% supports about ₹40.6 lakh over 15 years but about ₹49.7 lakh over 25 years. The trade-off is much higher total interest over the life of the loan.
Can I prepay my home loan without a penalty?
Floating-rate home loans to individuals carry no prepayment penalty under RBI rules. Fixed-rate loans may charge around 2-3% of the outstanding principal. Prepaying in the early years saves the most interest.
Is the 40% FOIR rule the same at every bank?
No. It is a common planning benchmark, but each lender sets its own FOIR limit and decides which obligations count. Treat any FOIR-based estimate as a starting point, not a guaranteed sanction.
