UPS vs NPS: Should Central Government Employees Choose UPS?

The Unified Pension Scheme (UPS) gives central government employees who joined on or after 1 January 2004 (about 23 lakh people) a choice. They can stay in the market-linked National Pension System (NPS), or move to a pension with an assured payout. UPS is administered by PFRDA as an option within NPS, not a separate replacement scheme. The switch is a one-way decision, so it's worth understanding both paths properly before choosing. This guide sets out how each works, compares them with worked numbers and explains who each suits.

How UPS works

  • Assured pension: 50% of your average basic pay over the last 12 months before retirement, once you have 25 or more years of qualifying service.
  • Shorter service: the payout is reduced in proportion to service below 25 years, with a minimum of ₹10,000 a month after at least 10 years of service.
  • Dearness relief: added on top of the assured pension, so the payout keeps pace with inflation.
  • Family pension: 60% of the employee's pension goes to the spouse after the employee's death.
  • Contributions: you pay 10% of basic + DA. The government contributes 18.5%: 14% to your individual account and 4.5% to a pool fund that backs the guarantee.

How NPS works

  • Market-linked corpus: you contribute 10% of basic + DA and the government adds 14%. The money is invested in equity, corporate bonds and government securities, so the final corpus depends on returns.
  • At retirement: you can withdraw up to 60% of the corpus as a tax-free lump sum. At least 40% must buy an annuity that pays a monthly pension for life. The pension is taxable at your slab rate.
  • Family pension: depends on the annuity type you choose at retirement. Joint-life annuities continue for the spouse.
  • Tax: the government's contribution, up to 14% of salary, is deductible under Section 80CCD(2) in both tax regimes.

Worked examples

UPS: assured pension

Say your average basic pay over the last 12 months before retirement is ₹80,000.

  • 25+ years of service: 50% × ₹80,000 = ₹40,000 a month, plus dearness relief.
  • 20 years of service: ₹40,000 × 20/25 = ₹32,000 a month, plus dearness relief.
  • Family pension: 60% × ₹40,000 = ₹24,000 a month to the spouse, in the 25-year case.

NPS: what the corpus might deliver

Now take a simplified NPS case. Combined contributions of 24% (10% + 14%) on basic + DA of ₹50,000 come to ₹12,000 a month, invested for 30 years. Using the future-value formula our NPS Calculator applies:

  • At 10% a year: the corpus is about ₹2.74 crore. Taking 60% as a lump sum gives about ₹1.64 crore tax-free. The remaining ₹1.09 crore in an annuity at 6% pays about ₹54,700 a month.
  • At 8% a year: the corpus is about ₹1.80 crore. That gives a lump sum of about ₹1.08 crore and an annuity pension of about ₹36,000 a month.

Two points stand out. First, a 2-point change in returns cuts the NPS pension by about a third. That's the market risk UPS removes. Second, these NPS figures keep contributions flat for 30 years. In practice pay and DA rise over a career, so real contributions and the corpus would be higher, and so would the last basic pay that UPS is based on. Don't compare a single NPS projection with a single UPS figure. Model a range of returns and salary paths.

Who should consider UPS, and who might stay in NPS

  • UPS tends to suit employees who value certainty: those with low risk appetite, those who expect to complete 25 years of service, and those whose spouse or dependants will rely on a stable, inflation-linked income. The 60% family pension and the ₹10,000 minimum are valuable protections.
  • NPS may suit younger employees with 30+ years ahead who are comfortable with equity exposure and disciplined about allocation. They may value the larger lump sum and the flexibility over how much of the corpus goes into an annuity.
  • Service length matters: if you're unlikely to reach 10 years of qualifying service, check the UPS rules for your case carefully, because the assured payout is linked to service.

Before you decide

  1. Confirm your exact qualifying service and current basic pay with your DDO (Drawing and Disbursing Officer).
  2. Check whether the option window is still open for you and what the deadline is. The choice is irreversible once exercised.
  3. Project your NPS corpus at several return rates (for example 8%, 10% and 12%) and compare the resulting pension with the UPS assured amount for your expected service.
  4. Think about your spouse's needs and other retirement savings such as GPF balances, PPF or mutual funds, which can offset the risk of staying in NPS.

Frequently Asked Questions

Who is eligible for the Unified Pension Scheme (UPS)?
Central government employees who joined on or after 1 January 2004, about 23 lakh employees. UPS is offered as an option within NPS, not as a replacement for it.
What pension does UPS guarantee?
50% of the average basic pay over the last 12 months before retirement, after 25 or more years of qualifying service, plus dearness relief. With shorter service the payout is proportionate, with a minimum of ₹10,000 a month after at least 10 years.
How does government contribution differ between UPS and NPS?
Under UPS the government contributes 18.5% of basic + DA (14% to the individual account and 4.5% to a pool fund), compared with 14% under NPS. The employee contributes 10% of basic + DA in both.
What family pension does UPS provide?
UPS pays 60% of the employee's pension to the spouse after the employee's death. Under NPS, family pension depends on the type of annuity chosen at retirement.
Can I switch back to NPS after choosing UPS?
No. The choice to move to UPS is irreversible, so confirm your service details with your DDO and check the current option window before deciding.
Who should consider staying with NPS instead of UPS?
Younger employees with 30 or more years of service ahead who are comfortable with market-linked, equity-heavy returns may prefer NPS's corpus route and larger lump sum over UPS's assured but fixed payout.
