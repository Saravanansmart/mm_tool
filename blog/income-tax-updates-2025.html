Income Tax Guide for FY 2026-27 (AY 2027-28): Slabs, Rebate, Old vs New Regime and Tax-Saving Deductions

FY 2026-27 (assessment year 2027-28) is the first year in which India's new Income-tax Act 2025 applies. The good news for salaried taxpayers is that the numbers you already know have carried over: the New Regime slabs, the ₹75,000 standard deduction and the ₹12 lakh rebate threshold are the same as in FY 2025-26. This guide pulls together the slabs for both regimes, the rebate, cess and surcharge, how to choose a regime, the deductions that still matter, and a fully worked example. To run your own numbers, use our Income Tax Calculator.

What is new for FY 2026-27

The Income-tax Act 2025 takes effect

The Income-tax Act 2025, enacted in August 2025, replaces the Income Tax Act, 1961 and applies from FY 2026-27 (AY 2027-28). Its purpose is to simplify the law rather than change how much tax you pay:

  • Fewer, clearer sections: over 800 sections are reduced to 536, organised into 23 chapters and 16 schedules, in plainer language.
  • "Tax Year": a single "Tax Year" concept replaces the earlier "Previous Year" and "Assessment Year" terminology.
  • Digital-first processes: expanded faceless assessments and prior notice before enforcement actions.
  • Clarifications: including on the standard deduction for house property, commuted pension, and the treatment of virtual digital assets such as crypto and NFTs as capital assets.
  • No major rate changes: existing slabs, deductions and capital gains rules continue. The New Regime remains the default.

Because provisions have been reorganised, section numbers in the new Act differ from the old ones. This guide uses the familiar references (80C, 80D, 24(b) and so on) because that is how most payroll forms and investment proofs still describe them.

Budget 2026 and salaried taxpayers

Union Budget 2026, presented on 1 February 2026, did not change the slab structure for individuals. For FY 2026-27, the New Regime slab table and the ₹12 lakh Section 87A rebate limit introduced by Budget 2025 continue to apply, along with the ₹75,000 standard deduction. Old Regime slabs and limits — including the ₹1.5 lakh Section 80C cap and Section 80D limits — are also unchanged.

Rebate, standard deduction and the ₹12.75 lakh figure

Under the New Regime, the Section 87A rebate reduces tax to zero when taxable income is ₹12 lakh or less. Salaried employees and pensioners also get a ₹75,000 standard deduction, so a salary of up to ₹12.75 lakh is effectively tax-free (₹12,75,000 − ₹75,000 = ₹12,00,000).

Just above the threshold, marginal relief applies: your tax cannot exceed the amount by which taxable income crosses ₹12 lakh. For a ₹13 lakh salary, taxable income is ₹12,25,000. Slab tax would be ₹63,750 (₹20,000 + ₹40,000 + ₹3,750), but relief caps it at the ₹25,000 excess, so you pay ₹25,000 plus ₹1,000 cess = ₹26,000.

The basic exemption is ₹3 lakh for senior citizens (60–80) and ₹5 lakh for super senior citizens (above 80). The Old Regime standard deduction is ₹50,000, and its Section 87A rebate (up to ₹12,500) applies only when taxable income is ₹5 lakh or less.

Cess and surcharge

A 4% health and education cess is added to the tax under both regimes. High earners also pay surcharge: 10% for income between ₹50 lakh and ₹1 crore, 15% between ₹1 crore and ₹2 crore, and 25% above ₹2 crore. The New Regime caps surcharge at 25%; the Old Regime has a 37% rate above ₹5 crore.

Worked example: ₹15 lakh salary, both regimes

New Regime. Taxable income = ₹15,00,000 − ₹75,000 = ₹14,25,000.

  • ₹4–8 lakh at 5% = ₹20,000
  • ₹8–12 lakh at 10% = ₹40,000
  • ₹12–14.25 lakh at 15% = ₹33,750
  • Tax ₹93,750 + 4% cess ₹3,750 = ₹97,500

Old Regime, no deductions. Taxable income = ₹15,00,000 − ₹50,000 = ₹14,50,000. Tax = ₹12,500 + ₹1,00,000 + ₹1,35,000 = ₹2,47,500, plus ₹9,900 cess = ₹2,57,400. The New Regime saves ₹1,59,900.

Old Regime, heavy deductions. Now assume ₹1.5 lakh under 80C, ₹50,000 NPS under 80CCD(1B), ₹25,000 health insurance under 80D, ₹2 lakh home loan interest and ₹1.25 lakh HRA exemption — ₹5.5 lakh in total. Taxable income = ₹14,50,000 − ₹5,50,000 = ₹9,00,000. Tax = ₹12,500 + ₹80,000 = ₹92,500, plus ₹3,700 cess = ₹96,200 — just ₹1,300 less than the New Regime.

The lesson: at a ₹15 lakh salary, the Old Regime only wins once deductions beyond the standard deduction exceed roughly ₹5.44 lakh (the point at which Old Regime taxable income falls to ₹9,06,250 and tax matches ₹97,500). Most people without both a home loan and significant rent will be better off in the New Regime.

Old vs New Regime: how to decide

  • Choose the New Regime if your salary is up to ₹12.75 lakh, or if your deductions are modest — its lower rates usually outweigh the lost exemptions.
  • Consider the Old Regime if you combine large deductions: full 80C, 80CCD(1B), 80D, home loan interest and a meaningful HRA exemption.
  • Switching: salaried individuals without business income can choose afresh every year at the time of filing. Taxpayers with business or professional income can switch back only once in a lifetime.
  • Re-run the comparison annually — a change in rent, a new home loan or a pay rise can flip the answer.

Key deductions and tax-saving options

Old Regime

  • Section 80C — up to ₹1.5 lakh: PPF, ELSS funds, EPF, NSC, life insurance premiums, 5-year tax-saving FDs and home loan principal.
  • Section 80CCD(1B) — extra ₹50,000: for your own NPS Tier-I contributions, over and above the 80C limit. See our NPS Calculator.
  • Section 80D — health insurance: up to ₹25,000 for self, spouse and children, and up to ₹50,000 for senior citizen parents; the total can reach ₹1 lakh.
  • HRA exemption: the least of actual HRA, rent minus 10% of basic + DA, or 50% (metro) / 40% (non-metro) of basic + DA. Our HRA Exemption Guide FY 2026-27 explains it in detail.
  • Section 24(b): up to ₹2 lakh interest on a self-occupied home loan.
  • Section 80E: full education loan interest, with no upper limit, for up to 8 years.
  • Section 80TTA / 80TTB: up to ₹10,000 of savings interest (below 60), or up to ₹50,000 of deposit interest for senior citizens.

New Regime

Most deductions are not available. What remains is the ₹75,000 standard deduction, the employer's NPS contribution under Section 80CCD(2) (allowed in both regimes), and Section 80CCH for Agniveer Corpus Fund contributions.

Practical tax-saving tips

  1. Plan early in the year. Spreading 80C investments monthly avoids a February scramble and gives money longer to grow.
  2. Don't buy products just for a deduction — if you are better off in the New Regime, the deduction is worth nothing.
  3. Ask about employer NPS. Because 80CCD(2) works in both regimes, routing part of your CTC there can lower tax either way.
  4. Insure your parents. A senior-citizen health policy unlocks up to ₹50,000 under 80D in the Old Regime.
  5. Check TDS on salary and interest so you claim the right refund when filing.
  6. File on time. Late filing can attract a penalty of up to ₹5,000 under Section 234F (₹1,000 if income is below ₹5 lakh).

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the New Regime income tax slabs for FY 2026-27?
0% up to ₹4 lakh, 5% from ₹4–8 lakh, 10% from ₹8–12 lakh, 15% from ₹12–16 lakh, 20% from ₹16–20 lakh, 25% from ₹20–24 lakh and 30% above ₹24 lakh, plus 4% cess.
Is income up to ₹12.75 lakh tax-free in FY 2026-27?
Yes, for salaried taxpayers under the New Regime. The ₹75,000 standard deduction brings ₹12.75 lakh down to ₹12 lakh of taxable income, and the Section 87A rebate reduces tax on that to zero. Marginal relief applies just above the limit.
Did Budget 2026 change the tax slabs for salaried employees?
No. For FY 2026-27, the New Regime slabs and ₹12 lakh rebate limit from Budget 2025 and the ₹75,000 standard deduction continue unchanged, as do the Old Regime slabs, the ₹1.5 lakh 80C limit and 80D limits.
What is the Income-tax Act 2025 and does it change my tax?
It replaces the Income Tax Act, 1961 from FY 2026-27, cutting over 800 sections to 536 and introducing a single "Tax Year" concept. It retains existing slabs, deductions and capital gains rules, so your tax does not change because of the new Act itself.
Which deductions are allowed under the New Regime?
Only a few: the ₹75,000 standard deduction, the employer's NPS contribution under Section 80CCD(2) and Section 80CCH for Agniveer contributions. Deductions such as 80C, 80D and HRA are available only in the Old Regime.
Should I choose the Old or New Regime?
Compare both. On a ₹15 lakh salary, the New Regime tax is ₹97,500, and the Old Regime only becomes cheaper once deductions beyond the standard deduction exceed roughly ₹5.44 lakh. Salaried taxpayers can switch every year when filing.
